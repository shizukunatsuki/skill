# Lecture Notes

Build one HTML document that teaches a topic — or, where the user wants a Word file, a PDF or slides, write its content by the rules in SKILL.md and let that format's own tool or skill make the file, as "Kinds of turn" sets out. The reader wants to understand the machinery, not to be convinced that a conclusion is true, and not to be walked through a proof.

The topic is not assumed to be mathematics. Linguistics, philosophy, history, music theory, computing and anything else are equally in scope. Nothing in SKILL.md is written for one subject: the rules are about explanation and representation in general, so read them for what they say and then pick the apparatus the actual subject needs.

## What the document is for

Three principles are the roots of everything SKILL.md says about explanation: "A lecture document presents a model", "Nothing carries value in itself" and "Grounding is the point". The rest of "What the document is for" works the three principles out; "How to explain" and "Structure rules" apply them. Delivery and process — which kinds of turn a request calls for, how the document gets built and checked, where resources load from, what the reply carries — stand on their own and are not derived from the three principles. When a rule about explanation looks arbitrary, or a case comes up that no rule covers, decide it from the three principles.

Four words are used throughout and mean one thing each. The **model** is the account the document works inside. The **mechanism** is what the model says makes the subject behave as it does — not restricted to causal or formal subjects: for a historical or philosophical topic it is whatever the model holds to organize the material. The **abstract account** is the statement of the mechanism at the general level. An **instance** is one concrete case of the mechanism. Four further words are used in their ordinary sense and are not synonyms for model, mechanism, abstract account or instance: the **object** is whatever the document is explaining, a **concept** is any single notion the account uses, a **constituent** is a part of the mechanism that the abstract account names, and a **term** is a named piece of established vocabulary — this subject's, or another field's where the reader holds that one, but never coined by the document. A term is the name; what it names is the thing. Contact is made against the thing, while the term is how the document refers to it, so "the terms a contact is grounded in" is shorthand for the pair and nothing turns on which half the phrase mentions.

**A lecture document presents a model, not the nature of its subject.** What a lecture document lays out was built by people, under the conditions they were in, to account for what they could observe. Whether the subject has a nature, and whether anyone could reach it, is not a question the document takes up or needs to. So say which model is in force and what it was built to account for, and mark where it stops accounting for things, rather than writing as though the account and the object were the same thing. Where rival accounts of the same material exist, name them in a sentence and say what the model in force was chosen for — presenting a model as the only one is a choice wearing necessity. The rival-accounts sentence goes at the end of the section that established the model, next to but not inside the boundary passage, which states where the mechanism stops and nothing else. The rival-accounts sentence is kept out of the opening because a defence of the choice at the entrance is a gate, and a sentence is the whole of it in any case.

**Nothing carries value in itself.** Value is never a property a thing has — it is brought in from outside, and whatever claims it has to show where it came from. Formal correctness does not carry value: a derivation that closes establishes that the conclusion follows from the premises under the rules, and nothing beyond that. Being more fundamental does not carry value. Being necessary does not carry value, so a definition presented as inevitable is a choice wearing value it did not earn. Having been established by someone does not carry value. Formal correctness, necessity and being more fundamental each have a prohibition of their own in "How to explain" — the bans on building a derivation, on presenting a definition as necessary, and on gating, which is putting a deeper layer in front of the content as a condition for proceeding. Having been established by someone governs "Quoted text is material the document works on" instead. The remaining prohibitions in "How to explain" break the core pattern or make no contact, and each is stated with the mechanism it breaks, apart from the three that "The prohibitions, and why they follow" marks as standing on their own.

**Grounding is the point.** An account is grounded when it has been brought into contact with something outside itself: a phenomenon it accounts for, a case where it does work, a difference it makes to what you would otherwise expect. The abstract account is what gets grounded; the instances are what ground it; a section is finished when it is grounded, not when it is complete.

**Grounding does not transmit.** A section assembled entirely from material that was grounded earlier is not thereby grounded. Value does not flow along a chain of use any more than it inheres in a form, so each section makes its own contact, even one whose every ingredient has already been brought down to earth. A document can satisfy every other rule in SKILL.md and still be a sequence of correct moves that never touches anything; "Grounding does not transmit" is what catches a document in that state.

**The ground is chosen, and it is whatever the reader already holds.** Contact works only against what the reader already has, so grounding is relative to them rather than absolute. What the reader holds is a set, not a tier, so there is no single depth to pitch at. What the document settles at the outset is the vocabulary it will express its contacts in, rather than letting it drift, extending it later only where a section deliberately introduces further terms and grounds them. The paragraphs from "What can serve as ground" through "Reaching material that nothing held has a counterpart in" work out what settling the document's contact vocabulary involves.

**What can serve as ground.** Two conditions bind together: the reader holds what the terms name, and this material has a genuine counterpart in what they name. Nothing is privileged beyond those two conditions — a register layout, the behaviour of a formal system, a mathematical structure the reader has already internalized all serve, and for material with no counterpart in ordinary experience there is nothing lower to reach for, since what the reader holds is all there is.

Terms the reader does not hold assume a grip they were never shown to have, so the section looks as though it rests on something and does not. Terms where the material has no genuine counterpart manufacture the contact, which is the metaphor case, and the test that separates a genuine counterpart from a metaphor is whether what is said about the concept stays true when read literally of the thing it is grounded in. A genuine counterpart carries the structure itself, so what is said stays true of it; a metaphor only resembles the structure, so parts come out false and the reader has to be told which parts not to carry over. Having to tell the reader which parts not to carry over is the diagnosis.

**Terms are always established vocabulary, never coined by the document.** Terms may be this subject's or another field's — whichever the term properly belongs to — but they exist already, with their established name and their established meaning, and what changes is that this reader now holds one, not that it now exists. Never coin a name, a framework or a classification to bridge a gap. An invented vocabulary is the metaphor failure in its worst form: a metaphor announces itself as a comparison, while a coined term presents itself as the subject's own and the reader has no way to tell that it is not. Where a gap cannot be crossed with vocabulary some field already has, say so in the reply rather than filling it. A term the document introduces is material the document teaches, so the term carries the same obligation as everything else the document teaches — check the name and the meaning as the field actually uses them, which is a fact like any other and falls under item 2.

**The instance is the contact; what its terms name is the ground.** Contact and ground are one act with two parts. Instances are new material the document supplies, which is what makes them contact rather than repetition; what has to be already held is what the vocabulary they are expressed in names. An instance stated in terms of register layout grounds for a reader who holds register layout, however unfamiliar the instance itself is.

**What the reader holds is always estimated, whether or not they said anything about it.** A stated baseline is evidence, and the heaviest available — never set aside for a weaker signal — but it is not a specification: people describe their own knowledge imprecisely, naming a field without its depth, or saying they know a little of something without a boundary. So run one procedure in every case, weighing what the reader stated, the vocabulary the request itself uses, what they have asked for before, and the level at which this topic is normally met.

Four rules follow, one for each situation: the signals agreeing, a stated baseline against the inferred ones, the inferred signals against each other, and no evidence at all. A single item in doubt inside an already settled baseline is a fifth situation and is handled under "Grounding in something and covering it are different acts", not by the four rules. Where the signals agree, what they agree on is the answer. Where a stated baseline conflicts with the inferred signals, the stated one stands — it is the only direct evidence there is, the rest being inference — and the conflict is reported rather than quietly resolved. Where the inferred signals conflict among themselves with nothing stated to settle it, assume the reader holds less, because the two errors are not symmetrical: grounding in something they do not hold fails silently and leaves them unable to say what went wrong, while grounding lower than necessary is merely redundant and visible enough to correct. Where the evidence runs out, two things are left: what the request itself assumes, and the level the topic is normally met at. Take the request's assumption where it makes one, and the prior where it does not — a bare topic with no other signal is the common case, and there the prior is all there is, so use it and say in the reply that it was a prior rather than anything this reader showed. Having nothing better is not a reason to have nothing, nor a reason to reach for something more elementary than the request's assumption or the prior. The four rules settle the baseline as a whole. State the baseline in the reply, with any conflict that had to be resolved, so it can be corrected.

A baseline pitched wrongly is the one error in estimating what the reader holds that costs a rewrite rather than an edit, and on a delivering turn the user does not see it until the document is finished. So the cost of a rewrite enters the same test the plan turn already uses: ask whether a document written at the estimated baseline would survive the estimate being wrong. Usually the document would survive — pitched a little high or low, most of it still reads, and the fix is an edit. Where the document would not survive, because the two candidate baselines would produce documents sharing almost nothing, the request has scope ambiguity of the ordinary kind and goes to a plan turn on that ground. Length plays no part in whether the document would survive a wrong estimate, and an unknown baseline still never triggers a plan turn by itself. Where the document would survive, write, state the baseline, and record it in the skeleton comment so a second attempt starts from something.

**Grounding in something and covering it are different acts.** Three consequences follow, and they are worth holding onto directly rather than re-deriving each time grounding and coverage meet:

- **Grounding constrains what sections make contact with, never what the document covers.** How deep the document goes is settled under "Depth is not rationed by usefulness"; a deeper layer simply gets its own contact in the document's terms like everything else.
- **The reader already holding something does not exclude it from coverage.** Holding something is what makes it usable as ground, and the same material can be both covered and used as ground. Which material is the subject's own is not a further judgement to make — it is the model the document works in and what that model was built to account for, fixed by the plan when writing and taken from the skeleton comment or the document itself otherwise. Inside the model and what it was built to account for, material is explained however familiar it is; outside them, material is brought in unexplained. Material inside or outside the model can serve as ground, because what qualifies something is the reader holding it, not which side of the line it sits on.
- **An item you are unsure of gets neither treatment.** Do not spend the document covering the item, and do not rest a contact on it. Write as though the reader knows the item, and ground on what the settled baseline actually rests on rather than on the item in doubt: something they stated outright; something the request shows them using rather than merely naming — a word appearing in a request is evidence they have the word, while applying it, reasoning with it, or asking something that only makes sense if its structure is in hand is evidence they hold the thing, and only evidence that they hold the thing will carry a contact; or, where the baseline had to come from the prior because neither of those exists, what that prior covers. Having written as though the reader knows the item, offer the background afterwards. Assuming familiarity governs coverage; grounding cautiously governs contact; each does its own job.

**The set grows as the document establishes things.** Once a section has introduced something and grounded it, later sections may express their contacts in it; that is ordinary cumulative writing and what dependency order is for. Expressing contacts in what the document established is the weaker option, because terms the document supplied are only as sound as the section that supplied them while terms the reader brought hold regardless, so prefer what they brought where the material allows. "Grounding does not transmit" and "The set grows as the document establishes things" divide the labour: "Grounding does not transmit" asks whether a section makes contact at all, and "The set grows as the document establishes things" asks what the contact is expressed in. A section making a genuine new contact in vocabulary an earlier section established satisfies both rules; a section making no contact of its own fails "Grounding does not transmit" however its terms were obtained.

**Reaching material that nothing held has a counterpart in.** Material with no counterpart in what the reader holds turns up as a document goes further from where it started, and such material does not get grounded by force. An earlier section introduces intermediate terms and the far material grounds in those. Introducing intermediate terms works because each intermediate sits between what the reader holds and the far material — it has counterparts in what the reader holds, so it can be grounded directly, and the far material has counterparts in it. Look for an intermediate between what the reader holds and the far material; where none exists there is no path, and the missing path is said in the reply rather than faked in the document. An intermediate the reader does not already hold has to be introduced, and introducing it is not covering a known prerequisite, since the rule against covering known prerequisites is about what they already know and this is by definition something they do not. The opening of the document also needs counterparts in what the reader holds: start where the material has them, and where it does not, start further back and say so in the reply. Each layer still makes its own contact; only the vocabulary changes, and the chain stays anchored at the reader rather than resting on itself.

The principles in "What the document is for" govern how the document is written; they are not its subject. Do not narrate the principles in the document — no passages about value not being intrinsic, about models rather than natures, about what grounding is. The only thing that surfaces in the text is the concrete form of "A lecture document presents a model": which model is in force, what it was built to account for, where it stops, and what the rival accounts are where there are any. Which model is in force and what it was built to account for open the section that establishes the model — normally the document's first, and wherever else it falls if not — and are not repeated at the head of every section. Where the model stops, and the rival accounts, both sit at the end of the section that establishes the model instead, placed as "Boundaries are separate" and "A lecture document presents a model" set out.

**Demonstration and rule-governed rewriting have equal warrant and different reach.** Neither demonstration nor rule-governed rewriting underwrites the other. What separates demonstration from rule-governed rewriting is what each extends to — a demonstration grounds what it shows and no further, while rewriting under stated rules reaches cases no one has observed. So mechanical transformations belong in the document, and they are there for their reach, never for warrant. "Do not build a derivation" forbids something else — argument structure, establishing that a conclusion holds — and the difference between rule-governed rewriting and a derivation is set out under "Do not skip steps in any mechanical transformation".

## Kinds of turn

There are five kinds of turn, and which of them a request calls for governs what gets produced, which checks run, and what the reply carries. Every later section that varies by turn refers back to "Kinds of turn" rather than restating the split.

Each entry opens with what identifies that turn. Reading the first sentence of each entry, together with "Requests that span more than one kind", is enough to route a request — the rest is for once the kinds are settled.

- **Delivering a document.** Writing a new HTML document or revising an existing HTML one. A file is produced, and the whole of "Verification before delivery" applies. A document in another format is a different kind of turn and does not fall under "Delivering a document" — see "A lecture document in another format".
- **Giving a plan for confirmation.** Whenever the user asks for a plan, an outline or a proposed structure; when "A lecture document in another format" sends a format route to the user; and otherwise only when the scope is genuinely ambiguous — the topic reads several ways and the wrong reading would waste the whole document. Size is never the trigger: the rules in SKILL.md produce long documents by design, so treating length as a reason to ask would put a round trip in front of every request. The criterion is whether being wrong is recoverable: pick the likeliest reading, and ask whether a document written for it would survive the user having meant another one. Where most of the document written for the likeliest reading would be reusable, write, and say in the reply which reading was written for and which were set aside; where the two readings share almost nothing, the whole document is at stake and the plan goes to the user first. Breadth often accompanies readings that share almost nothing, but breadth is not the test — a broad topic with one obvious reading needs no plan turn, and a narrow one that splits two ways does. The plan then bounds the document: it fixes what is covered, and material outside it is not added mid-write however relevant it looks. Terms introduced to reach planned material are not outside the plan — they are part of covering what was planned. Nothing is written to the output directory and no verification applies — there is nothing yet to verify. Once the user confirms, the confirmed plan is what was said in the conversation; the plan's working file in the scratch directory may not survive between turns — in the claude.ai sandbox it does not — so rebuild from the conversation rather than expecting the file to be there.
- **Working on runtime behaviour.** The document exists and the user has asked for the interface: interaction that misbehaves, layout, rendering glitches, appearance. The runtime round is the one "Verification before delivery" defers interface work to, and its budget is the opposite of the generation-time budget — run the page, operate every control, and do the code checks that only a running page reveals. Two things make the runtime round tractable, and both come from the prose "Figures" requires. The controls to operate are the ones declared in the prose "Figures" requires, which is the only list there is — a DOM sweep cannot see canvas-drawn or library-generated handles. And a control works when the figure does what the declaring prose says it does, whatever that is: a slider changes what the prose says it changes, a toggle switches what the prose says it switches, a drag driven through press, move and release leaves whatever is said to stay fixed unchanged while the rest moves. Drive a handle through pointer events wherever it has a DOM element to aim at, since a handle that cannot be dragged is exactly the defect a reader would hit and only the real event path shows it. Where a handle has no DOM element to aim at, fall back to the library's own API and say so in the reply: that exercises the state path and leaves the drag binding untested. Where the prose declares nothing in a document written under "Figures", that is a content defect and is fixed before the interaction is judged. Where the document was not written under "Figures" — an older document, or one from elsewhere — the absence of declared controls is not a defect: take the list of controls from the code, say in the reply that the list came from the code, and leave the prose alone unless the request touches it. The content rules still bind: a runtime round may not leave the content worse than it found it, and any content defect it turns up is fixed rather than traded away for behaviour. A file is produced, written as a revision is. Of the checklist, items 12 to 15 are re-run over the whole document and items 9 to 11 over whatever was touched; items 1 to 8 are re-run only if content was touched. The rule that a runtime round may not leave the content worse is checked whether or not content was touched, and when none of items 1 to 8 ran, checking the rule means exactly one thing: compare the text against what it was before the round and confirm that nothing was removed, reworded or regenerated except the content defects the round deliberately fixed, each of which is reported.

  When a turn needs both a delivering pass and a runtime pass, the delivering pass comes first, since the controls are operated on the page as it will be delivered: write and verify under the generation-time budget, then run the page and operate the controls. The runtime pass then produces no file of its own: it continues on the scratch copy the delivering pass built, and the single copy into the output directory happens after both. One reply covers the turn, carrying the delivering list in "What the reply has to contain" plus the runtime round's own items.
- **A lecture document in another format.** Asked for a Word file, a PDF or slides, or asked to revise one, the lecture-notes skill still governs what is written; that format's own tool or skill produces the file. The split is between content and file, and it applies the same way to writing a document in another format and to revising one. What governs: "What the document is for", "How to explain", "Structure rules", "Notation and representation", everything in "The document is one object" that is neither build mechanics nor an output or resource decision, what "Figures" says about when a figure is worth making and what it has to show, and "Language". What does not: everything about building, output, external resources and runtime, along with the build mechanics in "The document is one object" — the scratch and output directories, the skeleton, `data-section`, scratch appending, the shared script — and the machinery in "Figures", meaning interactivity, shared state and drawing through a library, since the target format decides what a figure can be, and a static figure there does not fail the interactivity "Figures" asks for.

  What holds for figures holds for any content rule the target format genuinely cannot carry: slides will not hold a contrast set side by side with a full transformation beneath it, and a deck that tries becomes unreadable. Where a content rule cannot be carried, keep the requirement's purpose and say in the reply which form it took and what was given up, rather than dropping it silently or forcing it. Of the checklist, items 1 to 8 and item 11 apply — notation saying what was meant is as much a question in a Word file as in HTML — and the rest have nothing to act on. Write the content in this turn, in the structure the plan settled, and pass it to the target format's own tool or skill in the same turn rather than describing what it should contain; say in the reply which rules did not apply. Where the host has neither a tool nor a skill for the target format, the turn becomes "Giving a plan for confirmation": survey what the environment does offer — a converter already present, or nothing — and put the route to the user alongside the plan, saying what it would consume and what it would produce, and wait. Nothing is installed to make a route possible and no converter is written: adding a dependency is the user's decision, and the rule against building things in "External resources, and not building things" applies to a converter as much as to a figure. Once the route is confirmed, the document is built as an HTML document under the rules for a delivering turn; a converter the user accepted runs on it afterwards, and where there is none the HTML document is delivered and the conversion is left to the user. Another format enters only when the request names one; the lecture-notes skill never proposes one on its own, and HTML is what it delivers otherwise. When revising, change what the request touches and leave the rest of the document alone — that principle is not HTML machinery, so it carries over however the target format's own tool or skill edits a document; regenerating the whole document and handing that over loses everything the request never mentioned.
- **Reporting a review.** The user asks for an assessment of an existing document and not for a change to it. Nothing is written to the output directory. The verification checklist still applies, but read against a finished document rather than one being written: item 2 checks the facts the document already states rather than facts about to be written, item 6 runs over the document as it stands rather than over what recent edits might have broken, and whatever items 12 and 13 turn up is reported rather than fixed. Items 9, 10 and 12 to 15 apply to an HTML document only: a document in another format has nothing to draw, render, load or resolve, so they are skipped rather than answered. Item 11 still applies — a Word file or a PDF has notation in it, and whether the notation says what was meant is as much a question in a Word file or a PDF as in HTML. The findings go in the reply; nothing is edited.

**Requests that span more than one kind.** A request can ask for more than one kind of turn at once. Judge, from what the request actually asks for, which kinds it contains and in what order they run, and carry out each as its own entry describes.

**The skill's own files are read-only guidance.** SKILL.md and `references/external-resources.md` are read and never written, whatever the host would permit. Editing either file is outside anything a request to the lecture-notes skill asks for: where the guidance is found wanting — a library that ought to be recorded, a rule that did not fit the case — the reply says so, as a proposed entry in the case of a library, and the change is the user's to make.

What the reply carries in each case is set out under "What the reply has to contain".

## How to explain

"How to explain" is the core of SKILL.md. Violating the rules in "How to explain" makes the document useless to the reader even when the content is correct.

### The core pattern: abstract, then concrete

The reader does not learn by being led to a conclusion. The reader learns by having an abstract account filled in by concrete instances — the abstraction states what the object is, the instances supply what it looks like, and understanding forms as the invariant across them settles into the abstraction. Filling the abstract account with instances is also how the account gets grounded: the instances are the contact with something outside it.

Write in the abstract-then-concrete shape, at the scale of the document, of each section, and of each paragraph:

1. **Open with the abstract account of the object, not with a conclusion and not with a label.** Say what the object is and how the mechanism works, at the general level, as fully as the general level allows — which includes naming its constituents and what each contributes, even those that later sections elaborate. A name followed by a promise to explain it later is not an abstract account; neither is a bare definition. The opening abstract account does not have to be absorbed on first reading — it will be incomplete for the reader until the instances fill it in — but it must actually be there, because it is what the concrete material afterwards is an account of.
2. **Then give several genuine instances of the mechanism the opening states.** Make the instances' surfaces as different from each other as the topic allows. Each instance must be self-contained: understandable without having understood the instance before it. Each instance is the abstraction made concrete in one particular way, so it must be traceable back to a part of the account given in step 1.
3. **Hold the mechanism fixed and vary everything else.** What the reader takes away is the invariant. So the mechanism must be literally the same thing in each instance, and the incidental features must genuinely differ between them.
4. **Let comprehension arrive late.** The reader tolerates not understanding the opening. What the reader cannot tolerate is material that is reachable only by having understood an earlier passage. Step 4 is about comprehension, not about material: a cumulative subject legitimately uses results established earlier, and that is what dependency order is for. The requirement is that every section has its own way in — its own abstract account and its own instances — so a reader who did not fully grasp the previous section can still enter the current one and get something from it.

An instance is never evidence. The general statement is given at the start and is not up for establishment; the instances show what it *is* and bring it into contact with something, not that it is true.

### What counts as a good instance

Not every correct example is a usable instance. Three properties decide which examples are usable instances.

**A graded or relational property needs a contrast set, not a single case.** When the concept names a dimension rather than a class, a single specimen does not show the dimension it lies on. Present together items that differ along the dimension the concept names, close enough to be compared at once. The difference between the items is the concept, and no single item carries it.

**Prefer instances where the concept is doing work over instances that exhibit it.** A case introduced as "here is an example of X" asks the reader to verify a match against a definition they have not grasped yet. A case where X has to be recognized in order for something else to make sense gives the reader a reason to find it. Wherever the topic allows, let the concept show up as a working part of a different question.

**A concept introduced once and then used later is not redundancy.** Give the concept's abstract account cleanly at its own place, without adding paraphrases to force it to land there, and let it recur in the later sections where it functions — at each recurrence, name it and restate its role in one sentence, so a reader who did not take it in the first time can still enter the section they are in. The one-sentence restatement covers vocabulary as much as concepts: when a section expresses its contacts in a term an earlier section introduced, restate in one sentence what that term stands for, at the term's first use in the section. Without the restatement, a section whose whole vocabulary came from an earlier one is reachable only by a reader who absorbed that earlier one, which step 4 of the core pattern rules out. Comprehension of an abstract property typically arrives at one of the later recurrences rather than at the definition. Introducing a concept once and using it later is distinct from splitting material into an appendix: the concept is fully introduced in one place, and what happens later is use, not deferred explanation.

### The prohibitions, and why they follow

Do not use metaphors, analogies or everyday-life comparisons. Do not anchor an explanation to one particular problem or worked case — treat the content as a general statement. Do not build a derivation. Do not present a definition as necessary. Do not build teaching out of deciding whether cases fall under a definition. Do not pad.

The six "Do not" sentences are the rules. Each one attaches value to something that does not come with it, breaks the core pattern in a specific way, or makes no contact at all, and the mechanism is what to reason from when a borderline case comes up:

- **A metaphor or analogy is a false instance.** A metaphor maps onto the mechanism only partially, so extracting the invariant across it and the real instances yields something that is not the concept. The damage is not that a metaphor is imprecise; it is that a metaphor actively corrupts what the reader extracts. The ban on metaphors and analogies covers explanatory comparison only. Established terms of art that happen to have figurative origins — tree, stack, pipeline, root, branch, field — are names, not comparisons, and are used normally; what is banned is reaching for something outside the subject to **stand in for** the mechanism. Grounding in something outside the subject is a different act and is allowed: there the mechanism is literally present in what it is stated in rather than resembled by it, which is why a mathematical structure the reader has already internalized can serve as ground while a comparison to something it merely resembles cannot. The literal-reading test under "What can serve as ground" is what separates grounding from a stand-in. Reaching for a stand-in is the move that a subject with no everyday counterpart invites, and the answer is never to manufacture one: ground in terms this reader actually holds, as "What can serve as ground" describes, rather than inventing a counterpart that does not hold up.
- **Anchoring to one particular problem or worked case leaves a single instance.** With no variation there is no invariant to extract, and the incidental features of the anchored case get absorbed into the concept.
- **A derivation is not a set of instances at all.** A derivation is a single sequence in which no step is independently accessible, so failing to follow one step makes everything after it inaccessible. A sequence in which no step is independently accessible is what the rest of SKILL.md calls a **verification chain**. Concretely, the ban on building a derivation rules out reasoning that only holds up if the previous step holds up — "it satisfies the defining conditions, and the object is unique, therefore they are equal", "it suffices to show that…", "hence it follows that…", "therefore it must be the case that…". Removing the word "proof" from the heading does not help if the body is still a derivation; the aversion is to the argument structure, not to the label. Several viewpoints on one mechanism are welcome, but each must reach it independently — no viewpoint may depend on another having been accepted.
- **Framing a definition as necessary turns it back into a conclusion** — something that has to be arrived at rather than simply stated and then instantiated. So present a definition or a convention as something that was chosen, and say what it was chosen for. No "inevitably", no "there is no other option", no "this is forced".
- **A classification drill makes no contact with anything.** Teaching built around deciding which cases satisfy a definition — sorting candidates, adjudicating membership, walking the reader through why this one does not qualify — exercises the definition against itself and grounds nothing. What the reader ends up able to do is sort cases, which is not what they came for. If a boundary case is worth raising, raise it where the mechanism stops applying, as the range of the model, not as an exercise.

  A classification drill is not the contrast set that "What counts as a good instance" calls for, and the difference is what the paired items are there to do. A contrast set is **shown**: the items differ along the dimension the concept names, they are placed together, and the difference between them is the content. A minimal pair in linguistics is exactly a shown contrast set and is entirely legitimate. A drill is **posed**: the reader is handed cases and asked to decide which side of the line they fall on. A contrast set and a drill can use the same material in opposite directions: the contrast set displays the dimension, the drill tests membership. Show the pair and say what differs; never ask which one qualifies.
- **Padding is repetition without variation.** Restating one instance in another form adds length without adding an instance. Several instances of one mechanism are required; several presentations of one instance are not. The test is whether the surface actually differs.

Three further prohibitions stand on their own rather than on the core pattern: no exam framing ("this is a tested point", "this comes up on exams"), since the reader studies out of interest; no coverage of prerequisites the reader already knows; and no unsolicited compliance, safety or environmental asides. The ban on unsolicited asides targets boilerplate moralizing attached to material that does not call for it; it never licenses omitting a hazard that is part of the subject. Where the material is a procedure that can injure, or a substance, dose or voltage whose danger is a property of the thing being explained, that danger is content and is stated as content.

An unclear prerequisite is never by itself a reason to stop and ask: it does not make the scope ambiguous, so it does not trigger a plan turn. Ask about an unclear prerequisite only when a plan turn is happening anyway; otherwise handle it as "Grounding in something and covering it are different acts" sets out for any item in doubt.

### Depth is not rationed by usefulness

Going deeper is not a fault. The document exists for understanding, and usefulness is not the criterion for where it stops — never cut a layer on the grounds that the reader will not need it, and never write that something is "enough for practical purposes" or "as much as you need to know". Cutting a layer as unneeded, or calling something enough, presumes a purpose the reader does not have.

What is actually forbidden is **gating**: placing a deeper layer before the content as a condition for proceeding, in the shape of "before we can talk about this, we first need to establish…". Being more fundamental is not a claim on precedence, and material placed in front as a gate makes comprehension of everything after it depend on comprehension of it, which is the same structural failure as a verification chain.

So the rule against gating is about position, not about permission. If a foundational layer is worth covering, cover it — as an object in its own right, in its own place, with its own instances, reachable by a reader who wants it and skippable in effect by one who does not, without being marked "optional".

The rule against covering known prerequisites governs a different body of material: knowledge from outside the subject that the reader brings with them. The rule against covering known prerequisites can never be the reason a deeper layer of the subject's own mechanism is left out — "they probably know this already" does not apply to material the document itself is explaining. Beyond keeping known prerequisites out, the rule against covering known prerequisites decides nothing: whether a deeper layer is worth covering is still settled by whether it introduces a mechanism not already given, not by how elementary it is.

The one thing worth cutting is regress that introduces no new mechanism: restating the same content in more primitive terms, without any new instance appearing. Regress without a new mechanism adds length and nothing else. The test is whether the deeper layer shows something, not whether the reader will use it.

### Boundaries are separate

Where the mechanism stops applying, point it out **separately**, as the range of the model. Separately means a short passage of its own after the mechanism and its instances have been given — not folded into the account, not carried as a qualification on every statement, and not moved to the end of the document where it becomes an appendix. The boundary passage states where the mechanism stops and nothing else; where rival accounts were named, the sentence naming them, as "A lecture document presents a model" describes, sits next to the boundary passage without being folded into it. The boundary passage belongs to the mechanism whose range it states, so it sits at the end of the section that presented that mechanism; a limit on the model as a whole sits at the end of the section that established the model — at its end, which is compatible with that section being the document's first, since the rule in "What the document is for" keeps the limit out of the section's opening passage, not out of the section. "Boundaries are separate" does not mean one boundary passage per section by default — a section that introduces no mechanism of its own needs none, and writing one anyway is padding. A section introduces a mechanism of its own when its abstract account states a mechanism that is not simply an instance or an elaboration of one already given. Consistency checking is never the main thread of the explanation.

### Density, not brevity

Aim for density, not brevity. Length is not a target in either direction. Do not treat shortness as a goal in a lecture document: "The core pattern: abstract, then concrete", "What counts as a good instance" and "Structure rules" call for several instances with different surfaces, contrast sets presented together, and every step of a mechanical transformation written out, and compressing those is how a document ends up short and useless.

Cut without hesitation: restatement of what was just said, derivational connectives, subsections created to hold one sentence, tables that repeat the prose, vague qualification that protects nothing, and recaps. Marking a specific fact as unconfirmed is not vague qualification and is never cut.

Do not cut, however long the document gets: an instance whose surface differs from the others, the second item of a contrast set, a step in a mechanical transformation, the role of a constituent in the abstract account, or the one-sentence restatement at a recurrence — of a concept's role, or of what a term stands for. The one-sentence restatement reads like a recap and is not one — it is the entry point into the section it stands in. When the impulse to shorten meets any of the material that is never cut, keep the material and let the document be longer. An explicit limit from the user is different from the impulse to shorten: it is a constraint on the document, so honour it, and say in the reply what had to be left out to fit — narrower coverage rather than thinner treatment of what is covered.

## The document is one object

Work on a part is always work on the whole. The attention needed to write one section is narrow by nature, and the standard failure is a document whose sections were each acceptable as they were written and which is uneven when read end to end.

**Plan the whole before writing any of it, and build it in passes.** The core pattern operates at document scale, not only section scale: instances of one mechanism are distributed across sections, and a section written without knowing what the others will carry cannot place them. A single write pass also front-loads the effort and produces exactly the decay "The document's quality is its weakest section" forbids.

Build in the scratch directory and copy the finished document across to the output directory once it is verified — "Output and delivery" fixes where each of the two is on each host — so that a run that fails partway leaves nothing half-written where the user would find it. The plan goes in a working file in the scratch directory too, and is never copied across, since it is not a deliverable; the reply states it briefly beside the delivered document. The plan settles seven things:

- which model the document works in, and what it was built to account for;
- the terms the document will express its contacts in, and the baseline they are pitched against — the reader's own terms to begin with, settled in the plan rather than drifting from section to section, and growing only where a section introduces further established terms and grounds them;
- the opening abstract account;
- which mechanisms recur, where their instances fall, and what each section makes contact with;
- which sections carry figures, and for each of those which library will draw it;
- whether the document will carry margin material at all;
- the section order by dependency.

**Read `references/external-resources.md` while making the plan**, under the rules in "External resources, and not building things". Settling which library draws each figure depends on `references/external-resources.md`. A figure with no library behind it reaches the user as a marked gap in the delivered document, or alongside the plan when a plan turn is happening anyway.

Then write section by section against the plan. Write the skeleton first — the head, the typeface and library loads, the styles, any shared script the sections will call, and the opening `<body>` tag left unclosed. Put the shared script at the end of the skeleton, after the library tags, so that under synchronous loading every library it calls is already defined when it runs. Load libraries with plain synchronous script tags, or have every inline script that calls a library — the skeleton's shared script where it makes such a call, each section's, and the final pass's — wait for `DOMContentLoaded`. Under synchronous loading a `defer` or `async` attribute on a library tag makes an inline script run first, against a library that is still undefined, and only the one-load check would catch it; where every library-calling script waits for `DOMContentLoaded`, `defer` is safe and `async` still is not, since an `async` library can finish after `DOMContentLoaded` has fired. The final pass is the script most easily left without the wait for `DOMContentLoaded`, because it sits at the end of the body and looks late enough already. The end of the body is not late enough: an inline script runs when the parser reaches it, while a deferred library runs only after parsing ends, so a `renderMathInElement(document.body)` call written plainly in the final pass runs against a function that does not exist yet. Anything a section's script calls goes in the skeleton, not in the final pass: inline scripts run in parse order, so a helper or a shared object written at the end would be defined after the sections that use it. Sharing is rarer than it looks — a helper used by one section belongs inside that section's own scope, and repeating a few lines in a second section is better than reaching for a shared block. Put an HTML comment at the top of the skeleton recording what the plan settled: the model the document works in, the terms its contacts are grounded in, the baseline those were estimated against, and, as each section is written, one line per section naming what that section makes contact with — which is what lets a later review judge each section's grounding instead of only its consistency. Keep the skeleton comment current: a section that introduces further terms adds them to it, and a revision that changes the model, the terms or the baseline rewrites it, so that what a later session reads is not a record of the first draft. The skeleton comment is invisible to the reader, so it is not the commentary that "Anything the grounding could not reach", under "What the reply has to contain", keeps out of the rendered page — "Anything the grounding could not reach" concerns what the rendered page says to the reader, and the skeleton comment says nothing to the reader at all — and the skeleton comment is what a later session reads instead of guessing. Without the skeleton comment, a revision in a fresh session re-infers the baseline from the document and can lock in an estimate that was wrong, with nothing left to catch it. Every piece of content is inside a section — the title block, a table of contents, an opening passage, a bibliography or notes at the end included; the ones that are apparatus rather than exposition carry `data-section` so they can be located, and "Grounding does not transmit" and item 4 do not reach them, since a table of contents has nothing to make contact with — so that the skeleton and the closing region hold no content at all. Each section is one element carrying `data-section` with a short slug as its value — fixed when the section is written, so that a later session locates sections the same way the current session did — which is what makes the skeleton and the closing region locatable; a document from elsewhere is read for the structure it already has rather than being restructured; where it carries no identifiable sections, add the attributes to what it does have; where there are no elements to carry them, wrap each run of content under its heading, which is restructuring and is reported as such. Then append each section to the end of the file the skeleton was written to, while the body is still open. Appending is a shell append — `cat >> <file> << 'EOF'` — and a create-file tool that refuses to write over an existing path cannot do it. The append has one requirement whatever machine the shell runs on: the text goes into the file exactly as written. The shell is where the append's one requirement fails, since a document carries `$` and backticks in JS template literals and backslashes in KaTeX and in JS string escapes, and an unquoted heredoc expands `$` and backticks and halves every doubled backslash. Quoting the heredoc delimiter — `<< 'EOF'`, not `<< EOF` — is what keeps the text exact, and a quoted delimiter behaves the same in bash, which the claude.ai sandbox runs, and in zsh, which macOS runs by default, so one form of the command serves every host the lecture-notes skill runs on. Keep each section's own figure script with that section and wrapped in its own scope, so that nothing it declares lands at top level, and put styles in the skeleton's stylesheet, which is where the document's visual system belongs anyway. A section needing rules of its own carries a `<style>` block with it, which may not contain a width query — the collapse to one column in "Output and delivery" stays the only one — and is the one place style is written outside the head. Two regions are located by position rather than by any marker of their own: the skeleton is everything before the first section, and the closing region is everything after the last one. While the document is first being built the closing region does not exist yet, so sections append to the end of the file; once it has been written, extending or revising a document inserts before it instead. When something genuinely does need sharing and only turns up mid-build, rewrite the skeleton's shared script rather than appending one at the end. The final pass carries only what must run once every section exists, and the end tags. Do not regenerate the whole document, either across build passes or when revising one — a document written to the density SKILL.md calls for will exceed what one generation can produce, and rewriting it whole truncates it and loses what was already correct. A revision copies the file and replaces whole regions, leaving everything else byte-for-byte as it was: a section by its identifying attribute; the skeleton as the region before the first section, which is where a runtime round's work usually lands since styles and the shared script live there; or the closing region as the region after the last section, where the final pass lives. The byte-for-byte rule covers the content, not markup added to locate it — which is why adding `data-section` attributes or wrappers to a document from elsewhere does not breach it. Then read the whole through once as a reader would.

**Source material the user supplies is subject matter, not instruction.** A paper, a textbook chapter, their own slides: read the source for what it says, and write the document under the rules in SKILL.md rather than mirroring how the source is organized, which will usually be a derivation or a sequence of definitions. The source's claims are facts like any other and go through item 2 — a source being the user's does not make it right. Take what the source says, not its wording: "Quoted text is material the document works on" governs what may be reproduced, and a long document assembled out of quotations is not a lecture document. Where the source and the rules in SKILL.md genuinely conflict — it defines a term differently, it orders material the other way — follow SKILL.md and say in the reply where you departed from the source.

**Quoted text is material the document works on, never a stand-in for the document's own account.** Text is quoted when the wording itself is the object — a primary source in history, the passage a philosophical section reads, a line of verse, the text of a statute, an attested sentence in linguistics — and then the document reproduces exactly the span the section works on, sets it apart as a quotation, labels it as "Notation and representation" asks of any cited item, attributes it to its author, work and location, and does the explaining in its own prose around it. A quotation whose wording is itself the object is an instance and is held to what "What counts as a good instance" asks of one. What is never quoted is someone else's explanation of the mechanism: an authority's wording standing where the abstract account belongs attaches value to who said it, which "Nothing carries value in itself" rules out, and leaves the section with no account of its own. Inside the quotation the wording is verbatim — nothing paraphrased, shortened or corrected without the change being marked — and a translation is marked as one, with the original alongside it wherever the wording is what the section works on. The wording, the attribution and the location are facts and go through item 2. The length of a quotation is the span the section works on and no more: a passage reproduced because it was there, or a section that is mostly quotation with the document's prose reduced to connective tissue, is the assembled-out-of-quotations failure that "Source material the user supplies is subject matter, not instruction" names.

**Revising starts from the existing document, not from a plan.** Revising from the existing document applies to any lecture document handed over, whether or not it came from the lecture-notes skill. For an HTML document, everything in "The document is one object" applies except its planning, which "Revising starts from the existing document" replaces, and "A review is a report, not an edit"; for a document in another format, see "A lecture document in another format" under "Kinds of turn", which says what carries over. Read the current file in full before changing anything and extract what is already fixed: terminology, symbol conventions, section order, which libraries and typefaces it loads, how deep it goes, and — from the skeleton comment where there is one, inferred from the document where there is not, with the inference stated as such — the model it works in, the terms its grounding is expressed in, and the baseline they were pitched against. The revision adopts what is already fixed rather than introducing a second set. Then state what will change and where, section by section rather than line by line — that statement is what takes the place of a plan for a revision, and it goes in the reply. Introduce a new term or a second library only when the existing one genuinely cannot serve, and say so when doing it. When a document from elsewhere conflicts with the rules in SKILL.md, change what the request touches, and report the other conflicts rather than silently rewriting the whole document to match. Of the checklist, items 12 to 15 are run over the whole document, since a load, an overflow measurement, a duplicate top-level name and a library call are properties of the page rather than of any one section; item 6 covers the whole document wherever it runs, by its own terms; and the rest are run over what the revision wrote. What the revision left alone is covered by "A change to one part is a change to the document", which is where the whole-document re-read and the mechanical consistency checks are set out.

**A review is a report, not an edit.** When the request is to review rather than to change, give the findings in the reply and write nothing to the output directory — do not edit the document under review and do not write a corrected copy alongside it. Offer to make the changes and wait to be asked, whatever format the document is in. "A review is a report, not an edit" applies because the user asked for a report, not because looking at a document forbids changing it: a request that also asks for changes is a revision, carried out as "Revising starts from the existing document" describes. A document in another format — a Word file, a PDF — is still reviewed, against the rules that "A lecture document in another format" says govern what is written; the ones it sets aside are HTML-specific, do not apply to it, and the report says so rather than reporting their absence as findings. Review against four things:

  - **Whether each section is grounded.** Name in the report the terms the document grounds its contacts in and the baseline they were pitched against, read from the skeleton comment where there is one and inferred from the document where there is not, and say which it was. Where the terms and the baseline were read from the skeleton comment, the review can judge the document against them, a baseline pitched wrongly throughout included; where they were inferred, the review can only find sections out of step with the rest and contacts in terms the document neither establishes nor could have assumed.
  - **The rules in SKILL.md.**
  - **The document's own internal consistency.**
  - **The external facts it asserts** — dates, quotations, attributions, the accepted definition of a term — checked against sources rather than against impressions.

  Work through the checklist in "Verification before delivery", read as "Kinds of turn" describes for a review, and give each finding as a location, what is wrong there, and what would fix it. Loading the document once is part of working through the checklist — items 12 and 13 cannot be answered otherwise, and nothing the load does reaches the file: item 13's pass drives the page's controls, which changes what is on screen and leaves the document on disk exactly as it was. The generation-time budget applies: one load, no operating the controls beyond item 13's pass over the native controls, and a note in the report if either a browser or the network was unavailable, since without the network the figures will not render and that is not a finding. Keep content defects separate from presentation ones, because they are not worth the same. A finding has to point at something concrete — this passage, this claim, this ordering — and name a rule where one applies. Where no rule applies, report the finding anyway, under its own heading rather than forced into one of the four review categories: a section that reads badly for a reason SKILL.md does not name is still worth knowing about, and pretending the four categories are exhaustive would only bury it. What does not go in is an unlocated impression.

**The document's quality is its weakest section, not its average.** Depth, the number and variety of instances, whether the section makes its own contact, the use of figures where the material calls for them, and the care taken over notation must not decay with position. "The document's quality is its weakest section" is not a demand for uniform figure density: a section whose material has nothing manipulable in it needs no figure, and some subjects carry a whole document that way. What must not happen is a section going without a figure, or without its own grounding, because the attention had run out by the time it was written. Before delivering, compare the sections against each other and find the weakest one; if it is noticeably below the rest, that is where the remaining effort goes, not into improving a section that is already good.

**Terminology and notation are global.** One concept keeps one name, one symbol and one convention throughout. A term introduced late must not be a second name for something an earlier section already named.

**A change to one part is a change to the document**, for edits to a document that is already finished — including ones the user asked for on a single section — and not for the build passes set out under "Plan the whole before writing any of it, and build it in passes", where the whole-document read happens once at the end. After an edit to a finished document, re-read the whole. Check that the edited part still matches the rest in depth, voice and terminology, and that it has not duplicated what another section already does. Then go through the mechanical consistency checks, which are the ones a local edit breaks silently: every reference to a symbol or term that was renamed; any count stated in the prose against the number of items actually present; section numbers against every cross-reference to them; and forward references, meaning a passage that has come to depend on something a later section establishes. A runtime round is held to a narrower form, set out under "Kinds of turn" — a before-and-after comparison of the text — rather than to the full re-read, unless it touched content.

## Structure rules

- **Order by dependency, not by mention.** The constraint is on what a passage *depends on*, never on what it *names*. A passage may not use a result, a formula or a property that a later section establishes — that is what leaves the reader stranded. A passage may freely name the constituents of the mechanism it is describing, including constituents that later sections elaborate.
- **Naming a constituent is introducing it, not referring forward.** The abstract account at the top of the document or the section is where its constituents first appear, and that is their introduction. When the abstract account names a constituent that is taken up later, give the constituent its role right there — what it contributes to the mechanism, where it sits relative to the others — and say which section elaborates it. A constituent named with no role attached is the hollow writing that "Naming a constituent is introducing it, not referring forward" is often mistaken to require; a constituent named with its role stated is exactly what the abstract account is made of.
- **Introduce notation where it is worked with, not where the constituent is first named.** At the abstract level, name constituents in words. A symbol is defined at the point it first carries content in a manipulation. Referents stay unambiguous throughout: no phrase whose object cannot be identified from what has been said, and no symbol standing for two things.
- Dependency order governs the sequence; abstract-then-concrete governs the content of each section. Dependency order is about inferential order and abstract-then-concrete about presentation order, so each section satisfies both at once.
- **Never split content into "appendix" or "optional / skippable" sections.** Related material belongs together in the same place.
- **Do not skip steps in any mechanical transformation.** Wherever something is rewritten by successive rule applications, show each application on its own line and name the rule applied. Each line does depend on the previous line's output, so what separates a written-out rewriting sequence from a forbidden derivation has to be stated: every line carries its own rule name and its own input, so any line can be checked on its own without reconstructing the chain, and the sequence asserts nothing about why anything is true — it records what was done, and it is there because it reaches cases a demonstration cannot. A rewriting sequence that does not name its rules, or that is arguing for a conclusion, is a derivation regardless of how it is laid out. A document *about* derivation — natural deduction, a proof calculus, the structure of an argument — is a different matter: there the derivation is the object being explained, and it is displayed, taken apart and shown working, like any other mechanism. What stays banned is using a derivation to carry the explanation.
- **Saying what causes what is not a derivation.** The ban on building a derivation targets chaining, where a step holds only because the previous one did. "X makes Y happen", "Y follows from the shape of X", "raising X lowers Y" are statements of the mechanism itself and are exactly what the document is for. Avoiding the word "therefore" while dropping the causal content is the wrong correction: keep the causation, drop the inferential scaffolding around it.

## Notation and representation

Notation is explicit and closed: every symbol is defined before use, every scope and range is stated, and nothing is left for the reader to infer from a pattern. Ellipsis is the standard way of violating explicit, closed notation in any field — it asks the reader to reconstruct a rule that was never stated — so state the rule instead, in whatever form the subject's notation provides for it.

Beyond explicit, closed notation, choose the representation the subject actually calls for. Where a field has an established notation, use it rather than inventing one, and say which convention is in force when several exist, together with the abbreviations it defines. Cited items are labelled and referred to by label. Structure is displayed rather than described in prose.

## Figures

A figure is worth making wherever the material has something with parameters or structure in it; where it has nothing of the kind, the section goes without one and that is not a gap. Where a figure is made, make it interactive wherever it is possible at all — drag handles, sliders, direct manipulation. When several panels show aspects of the same object, they share one state so that manipulating one updates all of them. The prose says what the reader can manipulate and what each control does — and, where the figure is presenting a contrast set, what stays fixed while the rest moves. The declaration of controls is not decoration: it is what the reader is told to look for, and it is the only list of controls a later runtime round has.

Anything with parameters or structure can be made manipulable, not only plots. Interaction is the most direct way to present a contrast set: the reader varies one thing and observes what stays fixed, which is the core pattern acting in a single figure rather than across several sections. Interaction is also the cheapest grounding available — a figure the reader can move is contact with something outside the account, made without leaving the page.

Figures go through a library rather than being drawn by hand; see "External resources, and not building things". A static diagram is a fallback, and a hand-written one is a last resort.

## Output and delivery

The bullets in "Output and delivery" are requirements, and some of them constrain *how* a typographic decision is made — which unit, which script's measure. What none of the bullets does is fix a **value**: no measure, size, spacing or colour is prescribed anywhere in SKILL.md. Values are part of the document, a badly set page damages content that is otherwise right, and choosing values is part of the work. Choose values on the grounds that they serve reading, decide once and deliberately, then return the attention to the subject rather than iterating on appearance.

- One single-file `.html` document written to the output directory and handed to the user by whatever the host provides for delivering a file. Where the output directory and the scratch directory sit follows from what the host designates, and the two are never the same directory. Where the host designates a place for deliverables, that is the output directory, and its own working location is the scratch directory: in the claude.ai sandbox those are `/mnt/user-data/outputs/` and `/home/claude`, with `present_files` as the hand-over. Where the host designates no place for deliverables — a Linux or macOS machine running the lecture-notes skill from a terminal — the lecture-notes skill creates one folder of its own in the working directory, `lecture-notes-work`, if it is not there already, and keeps both directories inside it — `lecture-notes-work/scratch` as the scratch directory and `lecture-notes-work/outputs` as the output directory — so that nothing the lecture-notes skill produces, finished or not, lands loose in the working directory, and a later turn finds everything in the one place. Where the host provides no mechanism of its own for handing a file over, the file's path stated in the reply is the hand-over. Everything in "Output and delivery" applies to the HTML case; a document in another format is produced by that format's tool or skill, and nothing in "Output and delivery" applies to it. **Do not publish the document as a hosted artifact**, whatever publishing mechanism the host offers: the file itself is what is wanted. "Do not publish the document as a hosted artifact" states a preference between available options, not a claim to outrank the host. Where the host offers publishing alongside a way of handing a file over, both options are available and "Do not publish the document as a hosted artifact" applies: hand the file over. Where the host instructs that documents be published, or offers no other way to hand a file over, follow the host — deliver by the mechanism it provides and say in the reply that a file was what the lecture-notes skill asked for, so the user knows what was traded. Ending a turn with nothing delivered is never the right reading of "Do not publish the document as a hosted artifact". Single-file, not offline-capable: libraries come from a CDN, so the page needs a network connection to open. A new document is named for its topic and never written over a file already in the output directory: where the topic's name is taken, the new document gets a name no existing file has, and never one ending in a revision suffix, which would make the new document read as a revision of the file already there. A revision is written to the output directory as a new file named for the original with a revision suffix — `<original>-rev2.html`, then `-rev3.html`, the number incrementing and never compounding. Take the next number by looking at what is already in the output directory, which persists across turns as the place deliverables live, rather than from the name of the file that was read, so that a resubmitted original does not collide with revisions already there. Where the name read already ends in a revision suffix, strip it before deriving the base name, so suffixes never compound. A runtime round writes a new file too, named by the same revision-suffix rule as any other revision. The revision-suffix rule keeps successive rounds orderable and distinguishable, and the file that was read is never written over — including when it already sits in the output directory, which a document delivered in an earlier turn will. A plan turn and a review write nothing to the output directory — a plan turn's own working file stays in the scratch directory, and anything a review's own checks need to write, such as a screenshot or the throwaway code for item 8, is written there too. See "Kinds of turn".
- **Desktop is the target.** Do not spend effort on narrow screens: no separate mobile layout, no breakpoint work, no touch-specific interaction. Do take the responsive measures that cost nothing — a fluid maximum width rather than a fixed one, text that wraps, containers that scroll rather than clip — so the document degrades tolerably on a smaller window instead of breaking. "Desktop is the target" means no extra effort for other platforms, not deliberate hostility to them.
- **The page is one prose column, with a margin column beside it when the document has margin material, and material breaking out across the full width.** Figures, tables and mechanical transformations take a width of their own, wider than the prose; the prose column stays at a measure that reads comfortably. The page gets a maximum width so it stops growing on very wide displays.
- **Horizontal space is judged by what it carries, not by how much of it is covered.** Empty margin costs nothing and is not a defect on its own. What is a defect is content that had to be stacked, deferred or dropped while the space beside the prose sat unused: a contrast set presented one item after another when its items needed to be compared at once; a figure and the passage discussing it not visible together; a wide table or transformation scrolling horizontally when there was room for it. The three defects are met by giving the material a width of its own and setting its items side by side — the breaking out that "The page is one prose column" describes, not the margin.

  The other kind of defect is a symbol legend, a term's gloss or a source reference forced into the flow of the prose when it could have sat beside it. A symbol legend, a term's gloss or a source reference that could sit beside the prose goes in the margin.

  The plan decided whether the document carries margin material. If the document carries none, give it a plain centred column and no margin column — a margin column that is empty throughout pushes the prose off centre for no return, and empty margin costs something exactly when the margin column as a whole is not earning its place. If the document does carry margin material, every row uses the same two columns, prose and margin, and a row with no note leaves its margin cell empty, which costs nothing because the margin column is earning its place elsewhere in the document.

  The margin carries what belongs *beside* a passage. The margin never carries something the prose itself owes: the abstract account that opens a section, a concept's definition, the one-sentence restatement at a recurrence — of a concept's role or of what a term stands for — and the boundary passage all stay in the flow, because each is something the reader must not be able to take for an aside and skip, and the rules requiring them fix where they go. Keep margin notes short: a note long enough to distort the row it sits in belongs in the flow instead.

  Build the margin as nested grids rather than as positioned elements. The page container defines the two columns, prose and margin. Every section is a `subgrid` of the page container, so it shares the prose and margin columns; and a block that carries a note is wrapped with its note in a row that is a `subgrid` of its section, the block in the prose column and the note in the margin column. The nested grids are what place a note beside its anchor without computing row indices, which would be the layout logic "External resources, and not building things" forbids. The note sits in the DOM immediately after the block it belongs with.

  A section's rows are its own top-level blocks — one row per block that can carry a note. A list or a run of paragraphs that belong together is one top-level block and so one row; inline elements are unaffected, since nothing below the block level takes part in the grid. Because every section shares the page container's columns, the prose column keeps one position and one width throughout and the text never shifts sideways from one paragraph to the next — the margin is a column that sits empty, not space the prose spreads into. In a document with a margin, what is centred in the window is the two columns together, bounded by the page's maximum width; material that breaks out may use both. The page is a single column with a margin beside it, not a two-column page: nothing but notes ever goes in the margin column. A note taller than its row extends the row, so notes cannot overlap and no collision handling gets written.

  On a window too narrow for the margin, collapse the page container to one column; each note then falls into the flow directly after the block it belongs with, because the DOM order is already the fallback order. The collapse to one column is the one place a width query is written, and it is the exception to "Desktop is the target".

  A note that turns up in a document planned without a margin goes into the flow; do not revisit the layout for it. Dropping a margin column the plan included is cheap enough to do: if a document planned with a margin ends up with too few notes for the margin column to earn its place — a handful across a long document leaves nearly every row's cell empty, which is the same cost as never using the margin column — drop the margin column, by an edit to the skeleton's stylesheet made alongside the final pass rather than as part of it.
- **Neither widening the prose column nor enlarging the type answers any of the defects under "Horizontal space is judged by what it carries".** Type enlarged at an unchanged measure carries the same words per line and shows fewer words at once, so enlarging the type raises the fraction of the screen covered while lowering the information on the screen. Base size is chosen for comfortable reading, and the measure for readability; neither is a lever for filling the window.
- Set the prose measure in characters of the script the prose is actually written in, never in pixels and never in a count carried over from another script. A CJK character occupies roughly twice the width of a Latin one, so a comfortable CJK measure is around half the character count of a comfortable Latin one. Prose that mixes CJK and Latin is measured by whichever script carries the bulk of it.
- Use the right CSS unit for the prose measure, because `ch` does not mean what it looks like it means: `ch` is the advance width of the digit zero, roughly half a CJK character, so a `ch` value chosen as a CJK character count yields a column about half the intended width. Use `em` for CJK prose and `ch` for Latin prose.
- Set `<html lang>` to the document's language, and load **every** typeface the document uses as a webfont from a CDN — in every document, not only when a particular face was asked for. The page opens on a machine whose installed fonts are unknowable when the document is written, so naming local families leaves the typography to chance, and the default fallback for CJK prose varies especially widely. The page already loads libraries from a CDN, so loading typefaces from a CDN adds no constraint that was not there already; the point is that the typography is specified rather than inherited from the reader's system. Fonts come either from a font service's stylesheet endpoint or from a versioned font package on a CDN; `references/external-resources.md` records checked families for both.
- No build step, no framework. Plain HTML, CSS and JS.
- Load only the libraries the subject actually needs. A document with no formulas has no reason to pull in a math renderer.
- **Verify every external URL by actually requesting it** before it goes into the file. Verification by request covers everything the page loads: library scripts, stylesheets, webfonts.
- **Pin an exact version wherever the resource carries one.** Library URLs carry a version — use the exact number, never a floating tag that resolves to whatever is current, and never a number written from memory. Some resources have no version to pin, font stylesheet endpoints among them; those are verified by request like everything else, they are simply not pinned.
- When the network is unavailable, nothing can be verified by request. Use what `references/external-resources.md` records — exact version numbers for libraries, and URLs with their parameters for font endpoints, which carry no version — since it is the only record of already-checked resources available offline. What `references/external-resources.md` records was true when it was written and may not be now, so each entry used offline goes in the unverified list described under "Verification before delivery", like anything else that could not be checked, rather than being reported as confirmed.

## External resources, and not building things

"External resources, and not building things" governs everything the page loads from outside itself — drawing and rendering libraries, and typefaces — and every HTML document needs typefaces even when it needs no library.

Building a component is not the task. Every unit of effort spent on a renderer, a layout algorithm, a parser or a drawing primitive is effort not spent on the subject, and the result is worse than the library that already exists.

**Never write your own** parser, typesetting engine, layout algorithm, or plotting primitive — for math, phonetic transcription, syntax trees, music, chemistry, graphs, or anything else.

**Read `references/external-resources.md` while planning, before deciding what figures the document will have, and again before adding a figure to a document that already exists.** Read `references/external-resources.md` for every HTML document, figures or not: every HTML document loads typefaces. Reading `references/external-resources.md` during planning is what makes it possible to know early that a figure has no library behind it, so the gap can be planned for.

`references/external-resources.md` records libraries and typefaces that have already been checked, a map from what a figure has to do to which library suits it, and the checked families and delivery routes for fonts. If `references/external-resources.md` is missing, say so in the reply and choose without it: libraries under the requirements in "External resources, and not building things", which stand on their own, and typefaces from a stable font CDN, requesting only the families and weights the document sets text in. `references/external-resources.md` is a starting point, not the permitted set: different subjects need different things, and a resource outside it may well be the better choice. Start from `references/external-resources.md` because it saves the research, then choose what actually fits.

Whatever **library** is chosen, whether `references/external-resources.md` records it or not, has to be a large, actively maintained project with a CDN build, with its version resolved and its API checked against that version's documentation. Typefaces are chosen against their own, shorter requirements, which `references/external-resources.md` states separately — they have no API and their endpoints often carry no version.

**Report every external resource the document loads** — libraries and typefaces alike — with its version where it has one and what it is used for. Mark the resources that are not in `references/external-resources.md` and give the reason each was chosen. No kind of turn edits `references/external-resources.md`, as "The skill's own files are read-only guidance" under "Kinds of turn" states: for each resource not in `references/external-resources.md`, put a proposed entry in the reply, in the shape "Entry format" in `references/external-resources.md` sets out, so the user can add the entry if they want to.

**When nothing suitable exists for a figure at all, ask — about the figure, not about the document.** Deliver everything else, leave a marked gap where the figure belongs, and say what the figure has to do and what was considered. Let the user decide whether to accept a different figure, go find a library, or drop the figure. Do not start building the missing component, and do not quietly substitute a weaker figure without saying so. One unresolved figure never holds up the rest of the document.

### Hand-written SVG is a last resort

Writing SVG by hand is expensive in output length and unreliable in geometry — coordinates get computed wrong, elements drift out of the viewBox, and nothing catches the errors. Reserve hand-written SVG for a static schematic with a handful of fixed elements. Anything with parameters, data, computed layout or interaction goes through a library.

## Verification before delivery

**Each check goes to whatever is reliable for it**, by the same division of labour that sends drawing to a library: judgement is the model's, exact computation belongs to a deterministic tool, and whether something rendered is the browser's to answer. Checking a number by reading it over is the same mistake as drawing a chart by hand.

What is not wanted is software-QA apparatus. No test suite, no `tests/` directory, no unit, integration or end-to-end layers, no mocks or fixtures, no coverage measurement, no CI configuration, no regression harness. A lecture document is not a maintained software product and does not get a software lifecycle built around it. Running a few lines of code to check a number is not software-QA apparatus, and it is required — items 3 and 8 both call for it.

The line for what counts as a defect is not code versus prose. **The question is whether a flaw changes what the document says.** A figure that draws the wrong object and a formula that renders a different expression from the one intended are content errors that happen to live in code. A slider that feels stiff is not a content error.

"Verification before delivery" applies in full to delivering an HTML document, with the scope each item runs over on a revision set out under "Revising starts from the existing document", and in part to three other kinds of turn, each set out under "Kinds of turn": reviewing a document, with the differences listed under "Reporting a review"; a lecture document in another format, with the items that apply listed under "A lecture document in another format"; and a runtime round, which re-runs the items "Working on runtime behaviour" names. A runtime round is the exception to the generation-time budget "Verification before delivery" sets out, rather than a case of it: operating the controls, which the generation-time budget keeps to item 13's pass over the native controls, is exactly what a runtime round exists to do. A plan given on its own has nothing to verify.

### Content

1. **Give correctness its own pass.** Reviewing formatting and UX is not reviewing content. Go through each statement, each transformation step and each concrete datum — numbers, cited examples, names, dates, glosses — individually, one at a time.
2. **Check every fact that could be misremembered or stale against a source**: dates, quotations, attributions, version numbers, the accepted definition of a term, who established what. Search rather than reconstructing from memory. When writing, check each fact before it goes in, and if it could not be confirmed, mark it as uncertain rather than stating it plainly and name it in the reply; when reviewing, check the facts the document already states.
3. **Check every computed result by executing something.** See "Computation — hand it to a deterministic tool"; item 3 is listed under "Content" because a wrong number is a content defect, not a technical one.
4. **Confirm that each section makes its own contact, in the document's chosen terms.** A section whose every ingredient was grounded earlier is not thereby grounded — name, for each section, what it touches: the phenomenon it accounts for, the case where the mechanism does work, the difference it makes to what the reader would otherwise expect. A section for which nothing can be named is the failure "Grounding does not transmit" describes, and no amount of internal correctness substitutes. Then check what each contact is expressed in against the terms the document settled on: the plan fixed them when writing; when revising or reviewing, they come from the skeleton comment where there is one and are inferred from the document where there is not, and a review names them in its report. A contact in terms where this material has no real counterpart is manufactured; one in terms the reader neither brought nor was given by an earlier section assumes a grip they do not have. A manufactured contact and a contact that assumes a grip the reader lacks both leave the section ungrounded for this reader.
5. **Review generated text, not just prose.** Strings built in JS, captions, `aria-label`s, tooltips and hint text all make claims, and they are part of the document.
6. **Apply "The document is one object" as a check, not only as a working rule**: name the weakest section, and run the mechanical consistency checks over the whole document. "The document's quality is its weakest section" and the mechanical consistency checks are both specified in "The document is one object", and item 6 exists so that they happen before delivery rather than being left to the moment of editing.
7. **Re-read against the core pattern and the prohibitions that follow it.** The failure to look for is a section that is factually correct and still violates the core pattern or one of the prohibitions — most often by opening with an instance or a bare label where the abstract account was owed.

### Computation — hand it to a deterministic tool

Every quantitative claim the document makes gets executed, not read over: numbers, closed forms, simplifications, limits, derivatives and integrals, counts, unit conversions, simulated results, and any value a figure is supposed to produce. Reading a result over and judging it plausible is not a check — that judgement is exactly what a language model is unreliable at, which is why computation goes to a tool.

8. **Run the computation.** Use a computer-algebra or numeric library; check a symbolic claim symbolically and a numeric one at several points. Where the environment has nothing that can run the check — a symbolic claim with no computer-algebra library to hand, most often — the claim goes in the unverified list rather than being read over and passed, since reading the claim over is the judgement item 8 exists to avoid. **Compute the expected value independently of the document's own expression** — evaluating the document's formula and finding that it equals itself catches nothing. Where the tool disagrees with the document, the document is wrong until something shows otherwise.

The code run for item 8 is throwaway: run, discarded, not delivered, not kept in the output directory, and not grown into a suite.

### The content carried by figures and notation

A figure asserts something, and so does a rendered formula; both are checked by reading the specification, not by clicking through the interface:

9. Read the drawing or layout code and confirm it computes the object the section is about: the right relation, the right axes and scale, the right structure, the right data. Evaluate the pure computation at two or three parameter values under item 8 and compare against values worked out independently.
10. Confirm the interaction expresses the right invariant. If dragging is meant to show what stays fixed, check that the quantity held fixed in the code is the one the text names — an interaction that varies the wrong thing teaches the wrong concept while looking correct.
11. Confirm the notation as written says what was meant: the source markup, not its appearance on screen.

### That the content is there at all — one load

A width or overflow mistake pushes content outside the viewport where it cannot be read, a figure that throws on load renders nothing, and notation whose renderer was misconfigured shows as raw source: each destroys content silently while the page still "loads fine", and none is caught by reading. So load the page once, with whatever headless browser the environment provides:

12. Confirm nothing is clipped, overlapped or pushed out of reach, and that none of the defects under "Horizontal space is judged by what it carries" is present: a contrast set split across scroll positions; a figure separated from the passage discussing it; a wide element scrolling when it could have been given a width of its own, which may reach the page maximum across whatever columns the document has; and, in a document with a margin, a symbol legend, a term's gloss or a source reference forced into the flow when it could have sat beside it. In a document planned without a margin, material sitting in the prose flow is not a finding — there is nowhere else for it to be. Load at a typical desktop viewport width and say in the reply which width was used, since the finding depends on it and headless defaults vary; if item 12 could not run at all, there is no width to report and item 12 goes in the unverified list instead. Check the rendered page rather than inferring from the source: compare `scrollWidth` against `clientWidth` on the document and on each figure container, or take a screenshot and look. Two things are not defects: vertical page scrolling, and a container that scrolls horizontally on purpose, which is the correct treatment for a wide table or a long formula line. What is a defect is content the reader cannot reach: the page itself scrolling sideways, or content cut off by a container that does not scroll.
13. Confirm every figure container holds a rendered figure, that no notation is left as raw markup, and that the console is clean. Some libraries render after the page has loaded — `references/external-resources.md` names them and the signal each gives when done — so wait for those signals before judging; notation still showing as source a moment after load, while such a library is at work, is not yet a finding. After item 12's measurements are taken — a reset or clear button changes the page, so nothing that measures the delivered state may run after the pass — and before reading the console, make one pass over the native controls, so that a callback which throws has a chance to say so: for each range and select, set a value different from the current one; for each checkbox and radio, flip `checked`, since their `value` attribute is fixed and setting it changes nothing; then dispatch both `input` and `change` on each of those, because a handler may be bound to either and a select's is usually on `change`; and dispatch `click` on each button. Changing the value before dispatching is what lets the pass reach a handler that only fails once the value has actually changed, and dispatching both events is what lets the pass reach a handler bound to `change`, which a bare `input` would have missed. A listener that throws does not make the dispatch throw — the error surfaces in the console — which is why the pass belongs in item 13, before the console is read, rather than as a check of its own. The pass costs a few lines and catches a control wired to a function that does not exist. The pass is bounded by what it cannot know: it has no idea what the figure is for — it can only tell a callback that threw from one that did not — so it reaches exactly those controls for which a valid input can be built without knowing anything about the document. Range, select, checkbox, radio and button qualify because the markup declares what is legal: `min`, `max` and `step`, the option list, the two states, and no value at all. Any other native control is taken on the same condition — the markup declaring what is legal — and judged one at a time: a number input whose `min`, `max` and `step` pin what it accepts is driven like a range, while a number input without them, a text input and a textarea are left alone, because what those accept is fixed by the subject the document is about rather than by the markup, and an invented input that a correct handler rejects would surface as a defect in a figure that has none. Leaving a control undriven is the safer of the two possible errors, because the two errors are not symmetrical: a control left undriven costs a dead handle the runtime round will find, while a defect reported against a correct figure costs that figure being changed to silence it. A drag and a canvas-drawn or library-generated handle are outside the pass for a different reason — a drag needs a press-move-release sequence, and such a handle cannot be enumerated from the DOM at all — and both wait for the runtime round.

What items 12 and 13 turn up are content defects, not polish. When delivering, fix the content defects and load once more to confirm the fix; when reviewing, report the content defects as findings and change nothing. The rule in "Static only — everything else" against chasing rendering glitches is about appearance; content that is missing or unreachable is not appearance. Otherwise the generation-time budget stands: one load, looking only at whether the content is there and reachable, and no operating the controls beyond the pass over the native controls that item 13 calls for.

**The one-load check depends on the network as well as on a browser.** The libraries come from a CDN, so in a sandbox without network access the figures will not render and the notation will show as source — which is the exact appearance of the failure being looked for. When the network is missing, do not count unrendered figures or notation showing as source among the findings, and do not quietly skip the one-load check either. Whenever a check could not be run at all — the browser missing, the network missing, or nothing available to execute a computation under item 8 — the reply does not describe the document as verified, and carries the unverified list: everything that could not be verified, unchecked URLs and versions included, item by item. The unverified list is one list for the whole reply, not one per section; its place in the reply is set out under "What the reply has to contain".

### Static only — everything else

14. Syntax is valid, no undefined identifiers, and no duplicate top-level names where several scripts share one global scope. The duplicate-name check is worth an explicit grep across every top-level declaration, because the two ways a duplicate name fails look nothing alike: a repeated `var` or `function` is silently overwritten and the page half-works, while a repeated `let`, `const` or `class` throws a SyntaxError that stops the whole second script from running.
15. Library calls match the version actually loaded, checked against that version's documentation rather than written from memory.

Keeping generation-time checking to one load and item 13's pass over the native controls leaves a known gap, and it is deliberate rather than an oversight: beyond the native controls item 13 reaches, an interaction delivered in the first version has been read but never driven, so its correctness rests on item 10 until the first runtime round settles it.

Beyond the one-load check and item 13's pass over the native controls inside it, do not exercise the controls, chase rendering glitches, or tune the interface while the document is being written. **Interface work belongs to a runtime round**, which happens when the user asks for it and carries its own budget — see "Kinds of turn".

### What the reply has to contain

Every item in the lists under "What the reply has to contain" is specified in its own section; the lists collect the items because they are easy to lose one at a time. Most items are conditional and most turns will owe only a few — say each owed item in a line or two, without headings, and leave out the items that do not apply rather than writing that they do not. Each obligation stays stated where it arises as well, because a section has to be usable on its own. Where a list item and the section it comes from disagree, do the union: an obligation stated in either place is followed. A disagreement between a list item and its section is not mentioned in the reply — the user is owed the items themselves, not an account of where they were written down.

What the reply owes depends on which kinds of turn the request called for, as "Kinds of turn" defines them, and a turn that carried out more than one owes what each of them owes. One item can arise on any kind of turn: **where the skill's own files fell short** — `references/external-resources.md` missing, or a rule in either file that did not fit the case.

**Delivering a document.**

- **The plan** for a new document, stated briefly beside it, including the terms the grounding is expressed in and, where the topic read more than one way, which reading was written for and which were set aside, so the user can correct either; or, for a revision, **what changed and where**, section by section.
- **Every external resource the document loads**, libraries and typefaces alike, with its version where it has one and what it is for — with the ones outside `references/external-resources.md` marked and their reason given, and a proposed entry for each in the shape "Entry format" in `references/external-resources.md` sets out.
- **The viewport width** the one-load check was run at.
- **The unverified list**, if anything could not be checked at all — the browser or the network missing, or no way to execute a computation. One list of everything that could not be verified, for the whole reply, not one per section.
- **What a figure needs and what was considered for it**, for any figure left as a marked gap because no library covered it.
- **The conflicts left in place**, when revising a document from elsewhere that does not follow the rules in SKILL.md and the request did not touch them; and any new term or second library introduced because the existing one could not serve.
- **Where the document departed from source material the user supplied**, because the source conflicted with the rules in SKILL.md.
- **What an explicit length limit had to leave out**, where the user set one.
- **That a file was what the lecture-notes skill asked for**, where the host's only way to hand the document over was to publish it.
- **An offer to add background**, where the document assumed a prerequisite was familiar and that assumption was not checked while planning.
- **The baseline the grounding was pitched against**, with any conflict in the evidence that had to be resolved, and whether it came from the prior rather than from anything this reader showed. For a revision, report the baseline recorded in the skeleton comment, against which whatever the revision adds was pitched.
- **Every fact left marked as unconfirmed**, so the user knows what to check rather than having to find the hedges in the text.
- **Anything the grounding could not reach**: material with no path from what the reader holds, an opening that had to start further back, a gap the field's own vocabulary could not cross. What the grounding could not reach is said in the reply and never in the rendered page, which says nothing to the reader about its own construction; the skeleton comment is not part of what the reader sees and is governed by "The document is one object".

**Giving a plan for confirmation.** The plan in full, since the reply is the only record the next turn has; the baseline the grounding will be pitched against, with any conflict in the evidence and whether it came from the prior; anything the grounding could not reach; and any question the planning turned up — a figure with no library behind it, a prerequisite whose familiarity is unknown, or a format route to confirm. The plan, the baseline, what the grounding could not reach and the questions the planning turned up are the items most worth correcting before anything is written. Nothing else from the delivering list applies: there is no document yet, so there is nothing loaded, measured or verified to report.

**Working on runtime behaviour.** What was changed and where, as for any revision; the viewport width; and the unverified list if it applies. Plus a statement of which interactions were actually exercised and which only through a library's API, which is what settles the gap that "Static only — everything else" leaves open; the result of the before-and-after comparison of the text; and, where the document declared no controls and the list of controls had to be taken from the code, a statement that the list came from the code.

**A lecture document in another format.** Everything in the delivering list except the two that depend on loading a page — the external-resource report and the viewport width — since nothing was loaded, measured or fetched. The unverified list still applies, since item 8 applies to a document in another format and a computation may be one nothing in the environment can execute; what the unverified list never carries for a document in another format is a browser or network entry, no page having been loaded. In addition: the content itself, handed in this turn to the target format's own tool or skill; a statement of which rules did not apply; and, for each content rule the target format could not carry, what form the requirement took instead and what was given up.

**Reporting a review.** The findings, and no document; the terms the document grounds its contacts in and the baseline they were pitched against, marked as read from the skeleton comment or as inferred; an offer to make the changes the findings call for, waiting to be asked, whatever format the document is in; and, for a document in another format, a statement of which rules did not apply to it. For an HTML document, two things from the delivering list still apply: the viewport width the review's load was run at, since the overflow findings depend on it, and the unverified list, since the review's own load can be blocked by a missing browser or network just as a delivering load can. For a document in another format there is nothing to load, so no width is reported — do not report one that was never used — while the unverified list still applies to a computation under item 8 that nothing in the environment could execute. Nothing else from the delivering list applies to a review, whatever format the document is in.

## Language

A new document is written in the language the user asked for, and in the language of the request when none was named. A document being revised keeps the language it is already in, whatever language the request came in — changing the language is a rewrite, not a revision, and is done only when asked for directly. The reply itself follows the user's language, for a new document and a revision alike. Code, identifiers, comments and configuration are always in English.
